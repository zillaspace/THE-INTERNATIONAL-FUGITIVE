The story

How it happened, in the order it happened.

This is the spine of the film, told the way the screenplay will tell it. Everything below comes from Anthony Marinello's account, the congressional record, and the press of March 2024.

FEB 29PORT AU PRINCE

A city eats itself

The gangs had a name for themselves now, Viv Ansanm, living together, and a spokesman in a bulletproof vest who called himself Barbecue. On the last day of February they stopped fighting each other and turned on the state. Two prisons opened in a night and four thousand men walked out into the dark. The airport took fire and the port closed. The prime minister, Ariel Henry, was out of the country and could not get back in; his plane was turned away and he sat in Puerto Rico while his capital burned on cable news. Eighty percent of the city belonged to men with rifles. The embassy flew its own people out by helicopter on the tenth and told every other American to shelter in place, which is a phrase that means: we are not coming.

In the hills above the city, in a walled compound called Have Faith Haiti Mission and Orphanage, forty children and a handful of American volunteers listened to the shooting move closer at night. One of the volunteers was Mitch Albom, who wrote Tuesdays with Morrie and had run the orphanage since the earthquake. They had been told there was no plan.

MAR 10 · 19:30FORT LAUDERDALE EXECUTIVE

The thirteenth company

Tropic Air Rescue had been open for ten days. Anthony Marinello had spent twenty eight years in the New Jersey State Police, twenty one of them in the left seat of a helicopter, and had flown the state's first medevac in 1988. He had built the first helicopter air ambulance the Bahamas ever had and then sat in a hangar in Fort Lauderdale waiting for an island to call. None did.

The phone rang at half past seven on a Sunday. It was Oscars night, and he had a houseful of friends in front of a new television, eating dinner, when the rescue line rang for the first time in its life. It was not an island. It was an aide in the office of Congressman Cory Mills, Florida's seventh district, a former 82nd Airborne soldier who had made a name flying Americans out of Kabul and Israel. Ten Americans were trapped at an orphanage in Port au Prince. The State Department had no plan. He laughed, because an hour earlier the news had shown prisons emptied and people burned in the street. "Who is this, for real?" It was real. Twelve helicopter companies had already said no; the pilot of the Red Bull helicopter had given them his number. Could he leave tonight. "Yeah, okay. We'll go." The aide, in shock: "You'll go?"

He had opened a business to pull sick people off beaches in the Bahamas, and his first customer turned out to be a war.

He left the party and called Benny Matos, the godfather of his daughters, the trooper he had flown with for thirty years. "Hey Benny, we got a job." "Where are we going, Bahamas?" "No. Haiti." Silence. Then Benny got in the car and did not tell his wife where he was going. At the hangar his vice president, Ed, said the thing nobody else would: "If you get killed in Haiti, it's over for everybody. You're the only one who knows what you're doing. The company's done." Marinello: "People call, we go. You don't always get to pick the ones you want to go on." Then he started taking things out of the aircraft.

MAR 10 · 22:30WHEELS UP

The weight of everything

An S76 will carry twelve people or enough fuel to cross an ocean, and it does not like to do both. He stripped the cabin. Stretchers, medical equipment, anything with a bolt, until there was nothing left but four seats and a floor. He ran the fuel to the gallon on a legal pad while Jordan, his director of operations, and Merrick, one of his pilots, built the paperwork for an island he had never landed on: Nassau, then the long black leg to Turks and Caicos, then Puerto Plata, then Santo Domingo, then over the mountains. His medical director, a trauma surgeon named Mauricio Lynn, told him it was suicide. His co pilot, Benny Matos, packed a Spanish phrasebook and said nothing.

There was no permit. "The first night, I invaded the country. There's no paperwork for that. We were a thousand percent illegal in everything we were doing." At half past ten a jet from New Smyrna Beach landed with the congressman and a retired Delta Force operator named Brian Young, and twenty thousand dollars in cash in a bag, because Marinello had told them that was what it would cost to keep an island airport open at three in the morning. They took no weapons, because a gun means jail in the Bahamas, in Turks and Caicos and in the Dominican Republic, so each man carried a knife.

"I don't care if they're lying on the floor. You want to live or you want to die?"

They lifted at half past ten with Florida behind them and nothing with a light on it anywhere ahead.

MAR 11 · 03:00PROVIDENCIALES, TURKS AND CAICOS

What it costs to keep an airport open

They had no night vision and no surface lights, only a five hundred foot ceiling and black water on every side, the kind of flying where the instruments are the only thing between you and the sea and the sea is patient. Five hours of it, broken by a fuel stop in Nassau where nobody asked questions.

Turks and Caicos was closed. Airports close at night in the islands the way shops do. The congressman counted twenty thousand dollars in cash onto a desk so the lights would stay on long enough to fuel. They fueled the aircraft, took the money, and went home. No paperwork, nobody in the tower. "We just left." A man who pays cash at a closed airport at three in the morning to cross a border on a flight plan nobody will admit to is, as far as any border guard can tell, a smuggler.

Saving a life had started to look exactly like smuggling one.

MAR 11 · 05:30PUERTO PLATA · SANTO DOMINGO

The day, and the prayer

Dawn came up over the north coast of the Dominican Republic as if nothing was wrong anywhere. They landed at Puerto Plata with no papers from the island they had just left, and the congressman waved a black passport and said "diplomatic mission" until the questions stopped. Too late to go in: "We're going to get shot to pieces if we go in there in the morning." A hotel on the beach, a few hours of sleep, a phone that never stopped. Over lunch Marinello chose the route alone and told no one, not even Benny, because the men in the back were texting the house in Port au Prince and a route that leaks is a route that gets you killed. He would stay away from the twelve thousand foot mountains to the north and go the long way instead, around the island to Santo Domingo, west along the coast, up a valley between two peaks, and down on the city like a hawk.

At three in the afternoon the congressman handed him the phone. A man from the State Department who never gave his name. "We know what you're doing down there. If you get captured or shot down, we are not coming for you. There'll be no rescue for you. You are one hundred percent on your own. We're going to disavow any knowledge of you being there." Marinello: "I wasn't expecting your help to begin with."

At half past seven: "Are we doing this?" "Yeah. What about you?" "I'm ready to go." Before they left for the airport he sat alone in a corner of the terminal with his head in his hands, flying the whole mission in his mind. "This was my whole life, right here. I got Benny's life in my hands." When he stood up, Benny, a deacon, and the congressman, a rosary tattooed on his arm, asked to pray. Four men joined hands in a Dominican terminal, and the terminal stopped to watch.

"This could be the end for all of us, or it could be just another night."

That evening, from Puerto Rico, Ariel Henry agreed to resign. The country they were about to fly into no longer had a government. They flew to Santo Domingo, where the authorities were already waiting.

MAR 12 · 02:45SANTO DOMINGO · THE VALLEY

Cleared westbound

They fueled at half past two with the airport authority and the army standing in a ring around the aircraft. Night helicopter flight is prohibited in the Dominican Republic. Marinello keyed the radio and asked to depart westbound along the coast below five hundred feet, and the only thing west of the Dominican Republic is Haiti. "You want to do what?" Then nothing. The whole frequency went silent. Then: "176 Bravo Alpha, you're cleared for takeoff, westbound." He looked at Benny. "Holy shit. They're gonna let us do this."

Along the coast to the go no go point. He waved the congressman forward: "Thirty one minutes. Tell them no bags, laptops only, passports. When they hear the helicopter, blink every light in the house on and off." Then he climbed to six thousand feet into the valley, two eight thousand foot peaks on either side, every light off, transponder off, tracking off, invisible to satellites, the iPad topography map on his lap and nothing but black out the window.

"If anything happens to me and I get killed, just keep going. If you get killed, I'm not gonna stop. Whatever we do, there's no getting captured."

67 SECONDSMAR 12 · 03:00 · PORT AU PRINCE

The lights

They broke out of the cloud at two thousand feet and the city opened under them like a wound, fires in the grid, smoke, no streetlights, the airport dark, and tracer rounds floating up through the smoke. He dropped to the rooftops at a hundred and twenty miles an hour, watching the GPS count down the miles. Eight. Seven. Six. At three tenths of a mile, one house in a city of a million began to blink. On, off, on, off. The volunteers inside were flashing the lights by hand, the oldest signal there is. He turned the aircraft sideways and matched it to a daytime photograph the State Department had given him. Benny: "You're gonna do a recon?" Marinello: "We're going straight in."

He slid down into the yard, landing lights on at ten feet, rocks and culverts and trees, and put the S76 down in a space the manual would not have approved. "Go." Benny, the congressman and Young were out before the skids settled. He sat at full power, waiting for someone to come through the bushes with a rifle. He had picked three in the morning for a reason: by three the men with the guns are drunk, and the rest are asleep. The glasses camera ran two seconds of someone sprinting toward a door in the dark. Ten people came out: Mitch Albom and his wife, and volunteers from Michigan who had come to a Haitian orphanage to help and had been told, when the city fell, that they were on their own. They climbed over the places where the seats used to be.

Sixty seven seconds on the ground. Fourteen people on four seats and a floor. He pulled it straight up over the tree in front of him, dove into the valley for airspeed, sucked up the wheels and shot back into the cloud, and the city went back to being a glow behind them. "It felt like ten seconds." They had fuel for exactly one airport.

MAR 12 · 03:45BARAHONA, DOMINICAN REPUBLIC

A closed airport, a caution light

On the way out of the mountains a hydraulic caution light came on. An S76 has two hydraulic systems; lose both and there is no recovering it, and the only thing that cuts a hydraulic line is a bullet. Barahona was closed too. He landed anyway, at a dark field in the southwest of the Dominican Republic, and two security guards came out speaking Spanish. Benny, who had said nothing all night, answered them in their own language: diplomatic mission, everything is fine. Then the rear door opened and twelve people climbed out of a helicopter with four seats, and the radios lit up the way radios do when a helicopter nobody scheduled appears out of Haiti with no manifest and fourteen people aboard. Police, army, customs, immigration, all on their way.

"Everybody against the helicopter. Let's take a picture before this gets out of control." Two photographs in the half light, the only images of that night most people will ever see: twelve people who had been in a war forty minutes earlier, a congressman, two pilots, and an aircraft with no seats. Then his phone rang. The congressman's office. "Did you hear what happened?" What could have happened in forty minutes? The Haitians had seen a blacked out helicopter come out of the mountains with the stars and bars on its side, the symbol he paints on every aircraft as a tribute to the military, and concluded the United States was attacking. The prime minister had resigned, and there was no government.

"You mean I single handedly overturned a government with a flyby?" "Don't say that again."

The embassy arrived, and the Dominican foreign ministry, and customs, and immigration, and everyone waved black passports until it was fine. Inside the terminal the congressman leaned over. "You know who that is? That's Mitch Albom. Pulitzer prize." Marinello had never heard of him; all night he had assumed "Mitch" in the texts was a security guard at the compound. "Ten people are ten people. Could be ten janitors." Outside, the man came over and stuck out his hand. "I'm Mitch." The hydraulic light turned out to be a loose switch. He checked the whole airframe for bullet holes anyway.

The credit

By breakfast the story was on the floor of a House committee hearing in Washington, told by a Michigan congresswoman. By lunch it was on cable. The congressman posted that his team and he had rescued the group, told a Florida outlet he had personally paid for the operation, and folded the night into a line about Americans abandoned by the White House. The press release counted the rescued and named the orphanage. It described the aircraft as a helicopter and pilot secured from the Dominican Republic.

The congressman had said to keep it quiet. He left Barahona with the State Department, flew to Washington and announced it on the House floor. Marinello and Benny slept in a fleabag hotel, flew home the next morning with Young in the back, and landed in Turks and Caicos to a woman behind the counter pointing at a television: "You're the guys." Fox News. The two photographs he had taken, on a loop. The caption named the congressman, named Brian Young, and called the men in the cockpit "the two pilots who helped." Marinello turned to Benny: "Did you carry the bags, or did you wash the windows?"

Two days after he put it in the refrigerator, he took his dinner out, heated it, and called the congressman. "Where do you want me to send the bill?" Mileage and a liftoff fee, the same rate he charges any Florida medevac, on nineteen hundred miles of Caribbean. Nothing for the five countries, the war, or the night. An hour later the phone rang. "This bill's outrageous. You're price gouging me. I'm going to report you to the attorney general." Then: "I'll pay you half." Marinello reminded him of the twenty five times he had said money is no object, told him where to put the bill, and hung up. "I'm the guy who kills the flies with the hammer. You will see this again." The congressman had told every news channel in the world that he paid for it. He has never paid a dollar. Florida paid for everything that came after. Tony never spoke to him again, turned down an invitation to go on CNN and say so, and told the people who asked that he would let karma handle it: "A guy like that, he's going to do it all himself." Then the investigations, and the primary. "You see what I'm talking about?"

"There's no price. It's never been done before. So that's the price."

A FEW DAYS LATERTHE RESCUE LINE RINGS AGAIN

Seven hundred

The rescue line rang for the second time in the company's life. "This is Governor DeSantis's office. We saw what you did in Haiti. We have seven hundred Floridians trapped in Port au Prince. Will you go back and get them?" "Do you know what I had to do to get ten?" He told them about the bill. "I can't get beat by Florida twice in one week." An hour later they called back: we will take care of his bill, charge us two or three times whatever you charged him, we need you to go back. He called Benny. "We got another call." "Bahamas?" "Haiti."

"You know I'm stupid enough to go back. That's not the question."

MAR 18 to 20CAP HAÏTIEN · THE UN LANDING ZONE

Pilot's discretion

Cap Haïtien is the one corner of Haiti the war cannot reach over the mountains. The airport authority and the civil aviation chief met him on the ramp: "You don't have a rescue permit." A permit, that is, to perform a rescue. The man in charge had sat in Marinello's office six months earlier trying to buy an airplane. Marinello would not sleep in Haiti: "You could kidnap me, you're going to take my helicopter." Every night back to Turks and Caicos, every morning back to the ramp. Tuesday, no permit. Wednesday, no permit. Florida's jets lined up on the apron, a security team moved families into safe houses in the capital, and Tallahassee called: we're spending millions here, why aren't you flying.

"You want some points on the scoreboard? Tell security to set up a landing zone. We'll be there in half an hour." On the runway the tower said it again: you don't have a rescue permit. "Are you going to take off at pilot's discretion?" "That's a really good idea." In daylight he landed at a zone in Port au Prince that turned out to belong to the United Nations. Fourteen women and children, strollers and suitcases thrown aside, people only. Then a second group arrived and grabbed Benny and started dragging him away, and Marinello, on a satellite phone to Tallahassee with fourteen women and children behind him and his hands on the controls, calmly explained that he was about to lift the aircraft and push a crowd with it. Benny reared back and punched the man in the face, ran, jumped in. "Go." He popped the collective and the rotor wash put the crowd on the ground.

A UN camera on the hill filmed all of it. The UN posted the half where the man runs from the helicopter, and the governor of Florida played it at a press conference, "our pilots are being attacked," while Marinello was still in the air and knew nothing. Cap Haïtien tower: "Negative. Circle the field. You took off illegally." "Clear the runway. We're coming in." Fifteen police met the aircraft. He raised his phone as if filming. The door opened, fourteen women and children stepped out, and the police stood down.

"We think you were the guys here three days ago, in the middle of the night. We have a picture."

Then: you are not leaving, because you took off illegally and landed illegally, and the fine is three thousand dollars. "How about this. I'll pay you three thousand dollars every time we take off and land, if you just let me keep doing rescues." No. Do it again and we confiscate the aircraft, arrest you, and put you in jail. He borrowed the cash from the captain of the last Florida jet on the ramp, counted out thirty hundred dollar bills on a desk, "probably the most of the GDP they've ever had on that side of the island," demanded a receipt and got one. Then out to the runway: departing northbound for Turks and Caicos. "Stand by." Ten minutes at idle. "Benny, do me a favor. Stare at that gate. One person, one vehicle moves toward us, you tell me, and we're out of here." The tower: "You're going to need to shut down and come back inside. We don't think you paid enough of a fine." He looked at Benny and started laughing.

"Yeah. Okay. Boom. We're gone. Five seconds, we're out over the ocean."

Four miles out the controller came up: you took off again without clearance. He did not answer.

MARCH ONWARDINTERNATIONAL FUGITIVE

The name

The next morning he landed back at Barahona, in daylight this time, and the whole airport came out: oh my God, you're back, we saw you on the news. Then: by the way, the Haitians are tracking you. They called us. They said those pilots are international fugitives, to be arrested on sight, and the aircraft confiscated immediately. Haiti, with no prime minister and a capital in the hands of men who had emptied its prisons, had found the bandwidth for one demand. The Dominican answer: "We hate the Haitians. We're not going to listen to anything they say. You guys are welcome here all the time."

In Tallahassee the state's emergency managers put a photograph of his helicopter on the board and wrote two words under it. From then on, every time Florida needed a helicopter: "Where's the international fugitive today?" He calls it the opposite of everything he has done in his life, and a badge of honor.

"Obviously, I know I'm not an international fugitive. But I guess I'm the international fugitive."

Then seven days in the Dominican Republic while the State Department, two embassies and a Haitian lawyer argued about a permit nobody had heard of. Home for Benny to serve Easter Mass. Another week and a half of daily Zooms. Then a tip: the head of Haiti's civil aviation authority was not in Haiti at all. He was holed up in a motel in Miami. Tony sent the address to Tallahassee. The Florida Highway Patrol and the Florida Department of Law Enforcement surrounded the motel, kicked in the door, and told the man he could sign the rescue paperwork or go back to Haiti right now. He signed. At eight the next morning, driving to work, Tony got the call: check your email. "There actually is a rescue permit."

He went back again. The officials at Cap Haïtien who had wanted him in jail met him on the ramp: welcome back, we know you have a permit. Three rescues the first day, then five a day, with a Dominican helicopter, two from the Canadian Armed Forces and a civilian that faded in and out, under a Florida contract run through a company called IRG. In about two weeks every one of the seven hundred was out. On the morning of April 15 he flew home to file his taxes; his accountant had already put him on extension, because nobody in the office expected him back in time. Those were the first two calls Tropic Air Rescue ever took, and he has not landed in Haiti since. Another Fort Lauderdale pilot who flew the island for twenty five years went back for abandoned airline equipment, was taken from his hotel at three in the morning, held for eight months on rice and candy, and released after the gang leader stared at him in silence for five minutes. He walked three nights to the border. His first call in Florida was to Marinello: "If you go back, they want you. You would be a prize." Marinello: "They probably have a price on my head."

The character

A cop on the wrong night, whose only weapon weighs five tons.

Anthony Marinello is the Die Hard hero rebuilt for a cockpit. Twenty eight years a state trooper, no patience for the people in charge, a mouth that gets him in trouble and a set of hands that gets everyone else out of it. The difference is what he reaches for. John McClane picks up a gun. Marinello picks up a Sikorsky, and he uses it the way McClane uses a building: every inch of it, in ways it was never designed for, until the problem gives up.

He has no weapon except the aircraft, and he gets fourteen people out on four seats.

Machines: how he fights

Every action beat in the film is a flying problem solved with the aircraft.

Weight

He takes out the seats, the stretcher and every bolt he can spare, because the fuel he saves is distance, and distance is what gets them home.

The ceiling

Five hundred feet of cloud over black water, flown on instruments the way a cop clears a stairwell, one step at a time.

The landing

Where there is no landing zone he makes one, in a courtyard the manual would never approve, and he is gone again in sixty seven seconds.

Fuel

Every stop is a countdown, Nassau, Providenciales, Puerto Plata, Santo Domingo, and if he misses one nobody comes home.

Cash

Twenty thousand dollars opens a closed airport at three in the morning, and he pays it for the same reason McClane throws a body out a window: it works.

The radio

Guards, towers, a government with no prime minister, and he talks his way past every one of them and lands anyway. The only weapon he ever draws is his voice.

It is why the role sells to a lead actor: a blue collar hero who wins with competence, and a machine most actors have never been inside. The S76 is his Nakatomi tower, and he knows every floor.

Feature architecture

Three acts inside forty eight hours, all of it in and around one aircraft.

The screenplay compresses the true events into the window between the 7:30 PM phone call and the 4 AM arrival in Barahona, with the rescue business, the certification fight and the crew relationships delivered as pressure inside that window. Every act ends at a border.

Format decision

Feature first, with the series built in.

Two products, one development spend. A feature is something the partnership can finance on its own terms: presales, a tax credit, a lender and equity, with the investor first in the waterfall. A series is a bigger business that a studio or streamer pays for in full, on their timeline. So the package is built as a feature, and the same sizzle, treatment and testimony are shaped into an eight episode bible at almost no extra cost. Buyers who want a film get a film. Buyers who want episodes see them in the same meeting. Either way the partnership sells once and gets paid for both: the feature through its own waterfall, and the series as a separate rights sale layered on top.

Feature: The International Fugitive

One night, five countries and 67 seconds on the ground, told as a contained true story thriller with 70% of its screen time in the cockpit, the cabin, a dispatch office and two tarmacs, which is what keeps a real helicopter movie at $12.5M. It is financed independently, through a sales agent, foreign presales and one name lead, and it is comparable in shape to Plane, 13 Hours, Argo and Greenland.

ROI path: credits and presales cover roughly 60% before equity is drawn; equity sits first in the waterfall with a 120% premium; the series rights are a separate revenue line carved out of the same package.

Series: Tropic Air Rescue

Eight episodes, with the founding of a rescue business as the serialized spine, a rescue a week as the engine, and the Haiti extraction as the season one climax. It is a premium procedural in the lane of Fire Country, Rescue HI Surf and the Taylor Sheridan founder dramas, with an ocean instead of a ranch.

ROI path: a studio or streamer finances everything; the partnership's return is fees, a backend and rights payments shared across the LLC. No equity required.

When it moves: pitched in the first quarter of 2028 with the sizzle and the bible, through a studio partner and a packaging agency. If a buyer orders it before the feature closes, the feature becomes the pilot film or steps aside.

Ten ways to protect the return

  1. Budget to the take numberThe sales agent's conservative estimate sets the ceiling. If the lead does not move the take figure, you change the lead and keep the budget.
  2. Equity draws lastPresales, the credit facility and in kind land in the production account before the first equity dollar is called.
  3. Equity first, 120%, then 50/50Every deferment sits behind the premium, which is what tells an investor the partnership is in the same position they are.
  4. One shoot, three usesThe January sizzle days produce the proof of concept, the cockpit plates for the volume, and the first frames of the trailer. Nothing shot in January is shot twice.
  5. Releases cost nothingMarinello is signed. Matos, Dr. Lynn and the flight crew sign a standard one page release for a credit. No story rights are bought from anyone.
  6. Keep the seriesThe feature financing agreement carves out series and sequel rights or prices them as a separate option. The world is worth more than one night.
  7. Tax structureSection 181 expired for productions that started after 2025. Bonus depreciation under 168(k) is permanent at 100% once the film is released, and a federal credit push is live in Washington. Structure the LLC so any renewal flows to the investors.
  8. Bond and weather coverA completion bond plus an aerial weather rider turns a lost flying week into an insurance claim instead of an equity call.
  9. Never one buyerForeign is sold territory by territory. Domestic is held until the film exists, then taken to four streamers and two theatrical distributors at once.
  10. Brand and impact money on topSikorsky, the Bahamas Ministry of Tourism, cruise lines and marine insurers all have a reason to spend on this story. It is marketing money, paid from revenue, so it never dilutes the waterfall.
Creative production solves

How a real helicopter movie costs $12.5M.

1

The fleet is the picture aircraft

Tropic Air Rescue's own S76s, hangar and flight crew come in as in kind equity. Marinello flies the picture ship himself. An aerial unit that normally leases and repaints aircraft becomes fuel, insurance and a coordinator.

2

The sizzle shoot is the plate shoot

The January days book the real aircraft over open water at night and along the Hispaniola coast. Add a gyro stabilized head and a plate list from the director of photography, and the feature's cockpit backgrounds are shot a year early inside the development budget.

3

Cockpit in a volume, cabin in a tank

Night interiors run in an LED volume pod on a motion base with the January plates. Cabin over black water plays in the horizon tank at Lantica in the Dominican Republic, which also books the 25% credit against stage days.

4

Port au Prince as one shot

The 67 seconds on the ground is one continuous take on a built orphanage courtyard, with the burning city as a ridge of practical fire and a matte. One set and one night, and it is the sequence the trailer gets cut from.

5

Barahona plays Barahona

The Dominican airfield where the real aircraft landed is a working location under the same film commission that issues the credit. The tarmac confrontation is staged where it happened, with stunt coordination and the real co pilot advising.

6

Real crew in frame

Flight medics, the dispatcher and the senior pilots play themselves or near versions, the way Sully and Thirteen Lives used real responders. It reads as authenticity on screen and as a press story off it.

7

Contained by design

Seventy percent of the script lives in six spaces: cockpit, cabin, dispatch, the Fort Lauderdale hangar, and two tarmacs. Scope is a writing decision made before a line producer ever sees the pages.

8

Shoot the sizzle

Three January days with the committed aircraft produce a scripted proof of concept before the script is finished. It is the single cheapest way to pull the whole schedule forward a year, and the footage becomes the trailer's opening.

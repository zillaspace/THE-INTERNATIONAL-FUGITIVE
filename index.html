One sheet

One night. One aircraft. One film built to be financed.

The International Fugitive is a scripted feature: a contained, 48 hour, true story extraction thriller in the lane of Argo, 13 Hours and Thirteen Lives. It is built to be financed independently for $12.5M, the all in cost of making the finished film, every dollar from the first day of prep to the day the master is delivered, before anyone spends on marketing or release. That number is covered with a name lead, foreign presales, the Dominican Republic's 25% transferable credit, and the real Tropic Air Rescue fleet as picture aircraft. The first move is a three day sizzle shoot in late January with the real S76 and crew, which is what gets a director, a sales agent and a lead on the phone before a page of script exists.

Where it stands: the life rights already cover scripted, series, sequels and remakes, and the existing agreement needs no rewrite. Tropic Air Rescue has committed aircraft, hangar and crew. The producers hire a production company and a director rather than directing in house. Marinello's rights are in hand at no cost to the partnership, and nobody else is paid for theirs. The crew sign a standard one page release for a credit, the same paper every true story film carries. The congressman is portrayed from the public record.

Format decision

Feature first, with the series built in.

Two products, one development spend. A feature is something the partnership can finance on its own terms: presales, a tax credit, a lender and equity, with the investor first in the waterfall. A series is a bigger business that a studio or streamer pays for in full, on their timeline. So the package is built as a feature, and the same sizzle, treatment and testimony are shaped into an eight episode bible at almost no extra cost. Buyers who want a film get a film. Buyers who want episodes see them in the same meeting. Either way the partnership sells once and gets paid for both: the feature through its own waterfall, and the series as a separate rights sale layered on top.

Feature: The International Fugitive

One night, five countries, 67 seconds on the ground. A contained true story thriller with 70% of screen time in the cockpit, the cabin, a dispatch office and two tarmacs, which is what keeps a real helicopter movie at $12.5M. Independent financing, sales agent, foreign presales, one name lead. Comparable in shape to Plane, 13 Hours, Argo and Greenland.

ROI path: credits and presales cover roughly 60% before equity is drawn; equity sits first in the waterfall with a 120% premium; the series rights are a separate revenue line carved out of the same package.

Series: Tropic Air Rescue

Eight episodes. The founding of a rescue business as the serialized spine, a rescue a week as the engine, the Haiti extraction as the season one climax. Premium procedural in the lane of Fire Country, Rescue HI Surf and the Taylor Sheridan founder dramas, with an ocean instead of a ranch.

ROI path: a studio or streamer finances everything; the partnership's return is fees, a backend and rights payments shared across the LLC. No equity required.

When it moves: pitched in the first quarter of 2028 with the sizzle and the bible, through a studio partner and a packaging agency. If a buyer orders it before the feature closes, the feature becomes the pilot film or steps aside.

The character

A cop in the wrong place on the wrong night. His weapon weighs five tons.

Anthony Marinello is the Die Hard hero rebuilt for a cockpit. Twenty eight years a state trooper, no patience for the people in charge, a mouth that gets him in trouble and a set of hands that gets everyone else out of it. The difference is what he reaches for. John McClane picks up a gun. Marinello picks up a Sikorsky, and he uses it the way McClane uses a building: every inch of it, in ways it was never designed for, until the problem gives up.

He doesn't carry a gun. He carries twelve people.

Machines, not guns: how he fights

Every action beat in the film is a flying problem solved with the aircraft. Nobody fires a shot who matters.

Weight is ammunition

Seats out, stretcher out, every bolt he can spare. The fuel he saves is the distance he can fly, and distance is life.

The ceiling is the enemy

Five hundred feet of cloud over black water. He flies it on instruments the way a cop clears a stairwell, one landing at a time.

The rotor is the door

Where there is no landing zone he makes one. A courtyard the manual would not approve. Sixty seven seconds.

Fuel is the clock

Every stop is a countdown. Nassau, Providenciales, Puerto Plata, Santo Domingo. Miss one and nobody comes home.

Cash is a key

Twenty thousand dollars opens a closed airport at three in the morning. He pays it the way McClane throws a body out a window: because it works.

The radio is the fight

Guards, towers, a government with no prime minister. He lies, talks, bluffs and lands anyway. The only weapon he draws is his voice.

It is why the role sells to a lead actor: a blue collar hero who wins with competence, not a body count, and a machine most actors have never been inside. The S76 is his Nakatomi tower, and he knows every floor.

Feature architecture

Forty eight hours. Three acts. One aircraft.

The screenplay compresses the true events into the window between the 7:30 PM phone call and the 4 AM arrival in Barahona, with the rescue business, the certification fight and the crew relationships delivered as pressure inside that window rather than as backstory. Every act ends at a border.

I

The Call · Fort Lauderdale to Nassau

Ten days of silence, then a congressman's office. Twelve refusals. Marinello strips the S76 for weight while the medical director argues the mission is suicide and the co pilot packs a Spanish phrasebook. The permit arrives by email. Wheels up 23:40.

Ends on the Nassau fuel stop: the last place anyone can turn around.

II

The Gauntlet · Turks and Caicos to Santo Domingo

Black water, no night vision, an iPad topography map. A closed airfield opened with $20,000 in cash. Puerto Plata at dawn. Santo Domingo: fuel, prayer, and the Delta operator who explains what happens if they are captured. "If I get killed, just keep flying."

Ends on the mountain pass into Haiti with the city burning below.

III

Sixty Seven Seconds · Port au Prince to Barahona

House lights blinking on and off. One continuous shot from the flare to the door closing. Over the trees, into cloud. Barahona: twelve people rolling out of a helicopter at a closed airport, radios screaming, a Dominican guard dragging the co pilot, and a punch.

Ends on the news: the prime minister resigns, the congressman is on television, and Haiti names the pilot an international fugitive. He goes back.

The story

The first call, the night, the credit, the fugitive.

This is the spine of the film, told the way the screenplay will tell it. Everything below comes from Anthony Marinello's account, the congressional record, and the press of March 2024.

FEB 29PORT AU PRINCE

A city eats itself

The gangs had a name for themselves now, Viv Ansanm, living together, and a spokesman in a bulletproof vest who called himself Barbecue. On the last day of February they stopped fighting each other and turned on the state. Two prisons opened in a night and four thousand men walked out into the dark. The airport took fire. The port closed. The prime minister, Ariel Henry, was out of the country and could not get back in; his plane was turned away and he sat in Puerto Rico while his capital burned on cable news. Eighty percent of the city belonged to men with rifles. The embassy flew its own people out by helicopter on the tenth and told every other American to shelter in place, which is a phrase that means: we are not coming.

In the hills above the city, in a walled compound called Have Faith Haiti Mission and Orphanage, forty children and a handful of American volunteers listened to the shooting move closer at night. One of the volunteers was Mitch Albom, who wrote Tuesdays with Morrie and had run the orphanage since the earthquake. They had been told there was no plan.

MAR 10 · 19:30FORT LAUDERDALE EXECUTIVE

The thirteenth company

Tropic Air Rescue had been open for ten days. Anthony Marinello had spent twenty eight years in the New Jersey State Police, twenty one of them in the left seat of a helicopter, and had flown the state's first medevac in 1988. He had built the first helicopter air ambulance the Bahamas ever had and then sat in a hangar in Fort Lauderdale waiting for an island to call. None did.

The phone rang at half past seven on a Sunday. It was not an island. It was the office of Congressman Cory Mills, Florida's seventh district, a former 82nd Airborne soldier who had made a name flying Americans out of Kabul and Israel. Ten Americans were trapped at an orphanage in Port au Prince. The State Department had no plan. Twelve helicopter companies had already said no. A short while later the congressman himself called to confirm it was a go.

He had opened a business to save people from the wrong island. His first customer was a war.

He said yes. Then he started taking things out of the aircraft.

MAR 10 · 23:40WHEELS UP

The weight of everything

An S76 will carry twelve people or enough fuel to cross an ocean, and it does not like to do both. He stripped the cabin. Seats, stretcher mounts, anything with a bolt. He ran the fuel to the gallon on a legal pad: Nassau, then the long black leg to Turks and Caicos, then Puerto Plata, then Santo Domingo, then over the mountains. His medical director, a trauma surgeon named Mauricio Lynn, told him it was suicide. His co pilot, Benny Matos, packed a Spanish phrasebook and said nothing.

The permit from Haiti arrived in his email while he was driving to the airport. In the back of the aircraft sat the congressman and a Delta Force operator with a camera in his glasses. Marinello gave the only briefing he gave all night.

"Under no circumstances will we be captured. If I get killed, just keep flying."

Wheels up at twenty to midnight. Behind them, Florida. Ahead, nothing with a light on it.

MAR 11 · 03:00PROVIDENCIALES, TURKS AND CAICOS

What it costs to keep an airport open

No night vision. No surface lights. A five hundred foot ceiling and black water on every side, the kind of flying where the instruments are the only thing between you and the sea and the sea is patient. Five hours of it, broken by a fuel stop in Nassau where nobody asked questions.

Turks and Caicos was closed. Airports close at night in the islands the way shops do. Marinello landed anyway and counted twenty thousand dollars in cash onto a desk so the lights would stay on long enough to fuel. He did not get a receipt. There is a word for a man who pays cash at a closed airport at three in the morning to cross a border without a flight plan anyone will admit to, and it is not rescuer.

Saving a life had started to look exactly like smuggling one.

MAR 11 · 05:30PUERTO PLATA · SANTO DOMINGO

Fuel and prayer

Dawn came up over the north coast of the Dominican Republic as if nothing was wrong anywhere. They fueled at Puerto Plata and flew on to Santo Domingo, and then they waited, because you do not go into Port au Prince in daylight. A day in a hangar with a stripped aircraft and a congressman on the phone. In the heat of that afternoon the Delta operator finally explained what happens if a helicopter goes down in a city run by gangs, and what he would do, and what he would not let happen to the people on board.

That evening, from Puerto Rico, Ariel Henry agreed to resign. The country they were about to fly into no longer had a government. After midnight they turned west toward the mountains and the border, with an iPad propped on the glareshield showing a topography map, because that was the navigation.

67 SECONDSMAR 12 · 02:30 · PORT AU PRINCE

The lights

Over the ridge the city opened under them like a wound, fires in the grid, no streetlights, the airport dark. They had coordinates for a compound and a city of a million people in which every rooftop looked the same. Then a house began to blink. On, off, on, off. The volunteers inside were flashing the lights by hand, the oldest signal there is.

He put the S76 down in a space the manual would not have approved. The glasses camera ran two seconds of someone sprinting toward a door in the dark. Ten people came out: Mitch Albom and his wife, and volunteers from Michigan who had come to a Haitian orphanage to help and had been told, when the city fell, that they were on their own. They climbed over the places where the seats used to be.

Sixty seven seconds on the ground. Fourteen people in a twelve seat helicopter. He pulled it into the air over the trees and into cloud with the weight of everyone's life sitting on the engines, and the city went back to being a glow behind them. Seventy minutes to the border.

MAR 12 · 03:45BARAHONA, DOMINICAN REPUBLIC

A closed airport, a punch

Barahona was closed too. He landed anyway, at a dark field in the southwest of the Dominican Republic, and the radios lit up the way radios do when a helicopter nobody scheduled appears out of Haiti with no manifest and fourteen people aboard. The guards came out. One of them took hold of Benny Matos and Matos, who had packed a phrasebook and said nothing all night, hit him. For a few minutes on a tarmac at four in the morning, the rescue was an incident.

Then paperwork. Then a group photograph in the half light that is the only image of that night most people will ever see: twelve people who had been in a war two hours earlier, a congressman, two pilots, and an aircraft with no seats.

MAR 12 · 08:00WASHINGTON · DETROIT · FOX NEWS

The credit

By breakfast the story was on the floor of a House committee hearing in Washington, told by a Michigan congresswoman. By lunch it was on cable. The congressman posted that his team and he had rescued the group, told a Florida outlet he had personally paid for the operation, and folded the night into a line about Americans abandoned by the White House. The press release counted the rescued and named the orphanage. It described the aircraft as a helicopter and pilot secured from the Dominican Republic.

The man who had stripped the aircraft, run the fuel math, paid the airport, flown the black water without night vision, found the blinking house and put the helicopter down in it, went back to a hangar in Fort Lauderdale with a company that was ten days old, an aircraft with its seats in a pile, and a bill. The calls that came next were from people who wanted him to do it again, and the help that had been promised with the cost, the paperwork and the exposure did not arrive. The credit had already been spent. The consequences were about to be delivered to a different address.

The hardest force to defend against is the one that arrives after the mission is over.

MAR 13 ONWARDINTERNATIONAL FUGITIVE

The name

Haiti, with no prime minister and a capital in the hands of men who had emptied its prisons, found the bandwidth to issue one demand: the arrest of the pilot and co pilot who had entered its airspace without authorization, landed in its capital, and left with its people. International fugitives. The Dominican Republic read the request and declined to act on it.

He went back. The Florida Division of Emergency Management contracted him to lead a multi helicopter task force, and over the following weeks the crew that had flown one night for a congressman flew it again and again for a state, pulling close to seven hundred Americans out of a country that had declared them criminals for the first ten. The name followed him on every flight.

Why the shoot is March 2028

Hurricane season ends November 30. Hispaniola's dry season runs December to April, and the real event happened in March. A mid March start lands the Port au Prince nights on the anniversary of the real ones and finishes before the rains.

Why the sizzle gets shot, not cut

Tropic Air Rescue has committed the aircraft, so the sizzle is three shooting days in late January: one night in the cockpit over black water, the 67 seconds at the door, and a tarmac beat, all with the real S76 and real crew. That is the reel a director and a lead actor say yes to in the spring, before the first draft is finished.

The four dates that make 2029

The development raise starts now and closes between the end of October and mid November. Crew releases signed by mid January. A shootable draft in October 2027. A lead with foreign value attached by EFM in February 2028. Hit those four and the film shoots in the March 2028 dry season and releases in 2029.

Ten ways to protect the return

  1. Budget to the take numberThe sales agent's conservative estimate sets the ceiling. If the lead does not move the take figure, you change the lead and keep the budget.
  2. Equity draws lastPresales, the credit facility and in kind land in the production account before the first equity dollar is called.
  3. Equity first, 120%, then 50/50Every deferment sits behind the premium, which is what tells an investor the partnership is in the same position they are.
  4. One shoot, three usesThe January sizzle days produce the proof of concept, the cockpit plates for the volume, and the first frames of the trailer. Nothing shot in January is shot twice.
  5. Releases cost nothingMarinello is signed. Matos, Dr. Lynn and the flight crew sign a standard one page release for a credit. No story rights are bought from anyone.
  6. Keep the seriesThe feature financing agreement carves out series and sequel rights or prices them as a separate option. The world is worth more than one night.
  7. Tax structureSection 181 expired for productions that started after 2025. Bonus depreciation under 168(k) is permanent at 100% once the film is released, and a federal credit push is live in Washington. Structure the LLC so any renewal flows to the investors.
  8. Bond and weather coverA completion bond plus an aerial weather rider turns a lost flying week into an insurance claim instead of an equity call.
  9. Never one buyerForeign is sold territory by territory. Domestic is held until the film exists, then taken to four streamers and two theatrical distributors at once.
  10. Brand and impact money on topSikorsky, the Bahamas Ministry of Tourism, cruise lines and marine insurers all have a reason to spend on this story. It is marketing money rather than money to make the film, so it never dilutes the waterfall.
Creative production solves

How a real helicopter movie costs $12.5M.

1

The fleet is the picture aircraft

Tropic Air Rescue's own S76s, hangar and flight crew come in as in kind equity. Marinello flies the picture ship himself. An aerial unit that normally leases and repaints aircraft becomes fuel, insurance and a coordinator.

2

The sizzle shoot is the plate shoot

The January days book the real aircraft over open water at night and along the Hispaniola coast. Add a gyro stabilized head and a plate list from the director of photography, and the feature's cockpit backgrounds are shot a year early inside the development budget.

3

Cockpit in a volume, cabin in a tank

Night interiors run in an LED volume pod on a motion base with the January plates. Cabin over black water plays in the horizon tank at Lantica in the Dominican Republic, which also books the 25% credit against stage days.

4

Port au Prince as one shot

The 67 seconds on the ground is one continuous take on a built orphanage courtyard, with the burning city as a ridge of practical fire and a matte. One set, one night, the sequence the trailer is cut from.

5

Barahona plays Barahona

The Dominican airfield where the real aircraft landed is a working location under the same film commission that issues the credit. The tarmac confrontation is staged where it happened, with stunt coordination and the real co pilot advising.

6

Real crew in frame

Flight medics, the dispatcher and the senior pilots play themselves or near versions, the way Sully and Thirteen Lives used real responders. It reads as authenticity on screen and as a press story off it.

7

Contained by design

Seventy percent of the script lives in six spaces: cockpit, cabin, dispatch, the Fort Lauderdale hangar, and two tarmacs. Scope is a writing decision made before a line producer ever sees the pages.

8

Shoot the sizzle, do not cut it

Three January days with the committed aircraft produce a scripted proof of concept before the script is finished. It is the single cheapest way to pull the whole schedule forward a year, and the footage becomes the trailer's opening.

Universal put Michael Bay on the true story of the Iran downed pilot rescue in May 2026, with a book arriving in 2027. Studios are buying real extraction stories again, and buying them early. That is the appetite this project sells into.

The streamer proof

Extraction 3 and the Mercenary series show Netflix treats extraction as a franchise word. The Rip shows Artists Equity can take a Florida true crime story to Netflix at a mid budget with a crew profit share. Both are the case that a $12.5M true story thriller has a domestic buyer at delivery.

The sizzle's own market

A shot sizzle with a real S76, a real crew and the actual story is a rare thing in a sales agent's inbox. It is the piece that gets a treatment read, and it is what the series pitch and the first streamer conversation are built on.

Distribution and when to bring it in

Sell foreign early. Hold domestic until the film exists.

  3. EFM, BerlinFebruary 2028Lead attached. Close the presales and the lender four weeks before cameras roll. The true story is in the trades by then, and the production is the news.
  4. Domestic, at deliveryEarly 2029Finished film to Netflix, Amazon MGM, Apple and Paramount+ at once, plus Lionsgate and Black Bear for theatrical. Competition sets the price; release lands in 2029.
  5. SeriesFrom Q1 2028Studio partner and agency pitch the bible with the sizzle. If a series order lands first, the feature becomes its film or waits.

Why domestic waits

A domestic presale caps the upside at the presale price. Holding domestic until the film is finished is how Thirteen Lives and The Rip found their number. The presales and the credit are what let you afford to wait.

What the sizzle does for the feature

The January reel puts the real story, the real aircraft and the real crew in front of buyers before a page of script is finished. Every meeting from Cannes onward opens with it.

The Story

Anthony Marinello, a 28-year New Jersey State Police veteran and career helicopter rescue pilot, bought a small island charter company, discovered there was no helicopter air ambulance serving the Bahamas, and built one from scratch. His very first call wasn't a Bahamian medical emergency. It was a request from a U.S. congressman to fly into the collapsing nation of Haiti, at night, through five countries, with no support, to extract 10 trapped Americans from a war zone. He said yes. He was the 13th company called. The other 12 said no.

The Opportunity

The project is one documentary, financed through picture lock, with the potential to be adapted later as a scripted feature (The International Fugitive). Distribution is deliberately open: YouTube, streamers, festivals, and hybrid paths are all live options to be explored once the cut exists. The visual world is extraordinary: turquoise water, Sikorsky S-76 helicopters, storm fronts, open-water flying, cockpit tension, medics working in tight cabins, and the constant friction of jurisdiction, bureaucracy, weather, and time. The protagonist is field-credible, cinematic, and real.

"This is not pilot heroics. This is system heroics."

The Plan

One Documentary. Nine Months. Three Trips.

This is not a development package waiting on a raise. Production financing is secured, all-in through picture lock, released in three tranches tied to three shoots. Life rights are signed. The protagonist flies real missions every week and the access is standing. The plan below takes the project from a signed agreement to a picture-locked documentary in nine months.

TRIP 1 · MONTHS 1–2
The Foundation Shoot
Small camera team. The high-level anchor interview with the principal subject, shot properly. This footage does two jobs at once: it is usable master material, and it is the sketch document for designing the full production, the locations, the visual language, and the complete interview list.
TRIP 2 · MONTHS 3–4
Principal Interviews
Full crew. The principal interview re-staged at master production value, plus every remaining core interview: crew members, the trauma surgeon, evacuee voices where cleared, and corroborating sources. The assembly edit begins from Trip 1 and Trip 2 material.
TRIP 3 · MONTHS 5–6
Visuals and Pickups
Aerials, aircraft photography, open-water and night-flight texture, locations and atmosphere, plus any pickup interviews identified in the edit review. Months six through nine carry the cut through picture lock.

The Structure

A single financier and rights holder funds the picture all-in through picture lock, in three tranches matched to the three trips. The financier recoups first from all revenues; thereafter, ownership and net proceeds are shared 50/50 between financier and filmmaker. Creative control sits with the director; any sale or distribution deal requires both signatures. Finishing beyond picture lock, color, mix, music, archival, and release, is scoped in a second round once the edit is underway.

Everything else in this deck (the comps, the pitch language, the festival route, the scripted feature posture) gets dramatically easier the moment the footage proves the world is as good on screen as it is on the page. Treat the rest of this presentation as the long game. Treat this section as the next nine months.

The Stakes

Why This Story Matters

In the Bahamas, someone has a stroke on the wrong island, and geography becomes the enemy. A shark bite on a remote cay. A cardiac event on a private yacht. A boating accident where the nearest hospital is hours away by boat and there is no helicopter coming. Before Tropic Air Rescue, the answer was a fixed-wing air ambulance that could take 8 to 36 hours to arrive, if it came at all. Patients had to be transported to an airport. Payment had to be arranged in advance. Insurance had to be confirmed. And the clock kept running.

Marinello's service exists to compress that geography. Sikorsky S-76 helicopters launch directly from Fort Lauderdale Executive Airport, staffed with certified trauma paramedics and nurses, carrying whole blood, defibrillators, and critical care equipment. A patient can be back in a U.S. trauma center within a couple of hours of the call. But between the call and the landing, the real story unfolds: a gauntlet of open water, weather, international borders, fragmented island governance, insurance obstacles, and a system that was never built for speed.

"The water between is the story."

This is about who gets saved, how fast, and at what cost when the map is working against you. It is a story about building a new infrastructure of survival in a geography that has never had one.

The Full Picture

Story Overview

Anthony Marinello spent 28 years in the New Jersey State Police, 21 of them as a helicopter pilot. He flew the first medevac of New Jersey's statewide helicopter medical response program on July 1, 1988. He flew governors and chased fugitives and pulled people out of wreckage for decades. He appeared in HBO's Succession as a helicopter pilot. By 2021, he was done working for other people.

He found a small FAA Part 135 charter company, Tropic Air Charters, that had been flying passengers and cargo between Florida and the Bahamas for 30 years. The owner wanted to sell but got cold feet. Marinello almost walked. COVID hit. The deal stalled, restarted, nearly died, and then closed. Marinello spent six months learning the business from the inside, sweeping floors and washing airplanes while the staff thought he was just a helpful friend. Then he bought it.

Within months, he expanded the fleet by 50%. Things were running well. Then one of his pilots called from a remote Bahamian island with a passenger having a stroke. No hospital. No ambulance. No helicopter. Marinello called 10 to 15 air ambulance companies. Most said no. One said it could get there by Sunday. It was Thursday. He found a private plane and got the man out on his own.

"I spent the next day researching the closest helicopter air ambulance in the Bahamas. Turned out there isn't one. There was never one."

The next morning, he told his VP they were changing the direction of the company. Two years of aircraft acquisition, FAA certification (the hardest designation to obtain, by his account), crew hiring, medical protocol development, and state licensing later, Tropic Air Rescue launched on March 1, 2024, as the first FAA-certified helicopter air ambulance dedicated to serving the Bahamas.

The phone didn't ring. For ten days, nothing. On March 10, at 7:30 PM, the call came. Not from the Bahamas. From a U.S. congressman's office. Ten Americans were trapped in an orphanage in Port-au-Prince, Haiti. The country was in civil war. Could he get them out?

Marinello was the 13th company called. The other 12 said no. He said yes.

He stripped the S-76 down to save weight, calculated fuel loads to the gallon, flew through the night across five countries with a congressman and a Delta Force operator in the back, navigated mountain passes using a topography map on an iPad, and descended into a burning city at 3 AM. The people in the orphanage blinked the lights on and off. He found them. He landed. Sixty-seven seconds on the ground. Everyone out. Over the trees, into the clouds, gone.

When they landed in the Dominican Republic at 4 AM, 12 people poured out of a helicopter at a closed airport. Immigration was not expecting them. The Haitian Prime Minister resigned. Marinello learned the flyby of his stars-and-bars-marked helicopter had been mistaken for a U.S. military incursion.

He went back. Multiple times. Contracted by the Florida Division of Emergency Management, he led a multi-helicopter rescue task force that ultimately extracted close to 700 Americans from Haiti. The Haitian authorities demanded his arrest as an international fugitive. The Dominican Republic refused to honor the request. The name stuck.

Back in the Bahamas, the real mission continues. Shark bites, boating accidents, strokes, cardiac events, golf cart crashes, yacht emergencies. Over 50 rescues in the first year. A crew of 30 full-time and 18 part-time employees. The 2024 Sikorsky Rescue Award. And a vision to expand down the entire Caribbean chain.

Dramatic Forces

The Antagonists

The antagonists of this story are not villains. They are forces. The thing that makes this material cinematic is that the danger is systemic, distributed, and relentless. There is no single bad guy. There is a network of obstacles that conspire against every rescue.

Force
The Sea
Open water between Florida and the Bahamas. No emergency landing zones. Engine failure over blue water at night is fatal. The ocean is indifferent and absolute.
Force
Weather
Tropical storms, low cloud ceilings, unpredictable squalls. A 500-foot ceiling and three miles visibility over an unfamiliar coastline at night. Weather doesn't negotiate.
Force
Time
Every minute over open water changes the outcome. A stroke patient loses brain tissue by the hour. A trauma patient bleeds. The clock is the villain that never stops.
Force
Distance
700 islands spread across 100,000 square miles of ocean. The wrong island can be the difference between life and death. Geography is the original antagonist.
Force
Fragmented Island Governance
No one cohesive group to speak to. Island-by-island variability in medical infrastructure, airfield access, and emergency coordination. Systems built for a world before helicopter rescue existed.
Force
Legacy Fixed-Wing Culture
Islands that have only ever known one method: get the patient to the airport. A helicopter that can land next to the scene is a concept they have to be educated on. Old systems resist new capability.
Force
Border-Entry Rules & Bureaucracy
Customs, immigration, general declarations, rescue permits that may or may not exist. You land at a closed airport at 4 AM with 12 people who didn't enter through normal channels and the authorities want answers. Paperwork doesn't care that someone is dying.
Force
Insurance & Reimbursement
Air ambulance companies that demand payment before dispatch. Insurance that doesn't cover international helicopter transport. The cost of readiness is enormous, but the cost of being uncovered is worse.
Force
The Government That Forgets
Once the cameras switch off and the politician has taken the on-air credit, the operators who actually flew the mission and absorbed the cost have a way of disappearing from the record. The hardest force to defend against, because it arrives after the mission is already over.

Operational Reality

Systems Friction by Jurisdiction

The Bahamas

Multi-agency complexity across a fragmented archipelago. No centralized emergency medical dispatch for helicopter rescue. Island-by-island variability in airfield infrastructure, medical resources, and communications. Many islands have never received a helicopter air ambulance flight. The existing system is built around fixed-wing transport to airports, which can add hours to a rescue that a direct helicopter landing could solve in minutes. Limited healthcare infrastructure on smaller islands. Reimbursement models designed for a different era of patient transport. Educating local authorities, homeowners associations, and medical providers about helicopter capability is an ongoing process that runs in parallel with actual rescue operations.

The United States

FAA certification for helicopter air ambulance operations is, by Marinello's account, the hardest aspect to obtain. COPCN (Certificate of Public Convenience and Necessity) laws in Florida prevent private helicopter ambulances from responding to emergency scenes within the state, as counties hold those designations for their own EMS programs. Cross-border re-entry with medically urgent patients creates documentation challenges. Insurance and medical eligibility create friction at the receiving end. Aviation regulatory burden in cross-border operations adds layers of compliance to every flight.

Third-Country / International

Patients who are neither U.S. citizens nor Bahamian nationals create a third layer of complexity: entry documentation, visa status, passport validity, insurance coverage, and medical admissibility. In the Haiti operation, the challenge was extracting Americans from a country with a collapsing government, transiting through the Dominican Republic, and getting them processed through immigration at closed airports in the middle of the night. Rescue permits were invented on the fly. The international dimension is not an edge case. It is a recurring operational reality.

Market Context

Why Now

Audiences are responding to real operational stories with mission structure and procedural credibility. Premium rescue documentaries are performing across platforms. Cross-border dysfunction, healthcare access, and emergency infrastructure are resonant contemporary themes. The contrast between paradise and emergency is one of the strongest visual and emotional hooks available: the same turquoise water that sells vacation packages becomes a life-threatening obstacle when someone has a cardiac event on the wrong island.

Short-form and long-form platforms both reward mission-based storytelling with recurring casts and built-in stakes. Aviation content continues to perform well across digital audiences. The geopolitical dimension (Haiti, border sovereignty, fragmented governance) adds depth and urgency beyond what a domestic rescue story can achieve. And the founder is alive, active, operational, and available, a rare alignment of access and dramatic material.

Visual World

The imagery of this project is its own argument. The visual language is defined by contrast: beauty and danger, paradise and emergency, turquoise calm and operational urgency. Every frame should feel like it could be a poster.

The Water Between

The Black Water Problem

Engine failure over open water at night, with no NVGs, no surface lights, and a 500-foot ceiling, is the kind of scenario that makes commercial aviators say no. Marinello's crew said yes, repeatedly, because the alternative was someone bleeding out on a beach, or burning in an orphanage, or stuck on the wrong side of an ocean that does not care.

What the Audience Sees

A cockpit lit only by instruments. The horizon line gone. Comms chatter that sounds like air-traffic poetry. A pilot who has done this five thousand times before, doing the calculation again, in real time. And below the helicopter: nothing. Just water. Just distance. Just the clock.

Human Stakes

The People in the Back

When a helicopter lands at a closed airport in the middle of the night and the door pops open, the camera should not be on the pilot. It should be on the people stepping out. This is the heart of the project: not the aircraft, not the geopolitics, but the human cargo that motivated the entire flight.

Editorial Posture

The project will not exploit the people Marinello rescued. It will treat them as protagonists in their own right, not as visual texture. Where rights and consent allow, real footage and testimony will be used. Where they don't, the editorial work will lean on negative space, off-camera audio, environmental detail, and reconstruction that is openly labeled as such.

The Test

Before any footage of a rescued person reaches a final cut, three questions: Did they consent in writing, knowing exactly how the material will be used? Does the framing protect their identity if they want it protected? Is the footage doing emotional work that text and B-roll cannot do alone? If any of those answers is no, the footage doesn't go in.

Production Economics

Budget Posture

The material can be produced credibly across three production tiers. Each tier preserves the operational integrity of the story while calibrating spectacle, talent, and distribution ambition. The scripted feature is built to scale.

Tier 1 · Lean
$3M to $8M

Indie thriller posture. Festival-circuit ambitions. One or two recognizable lead casting. Helicopter cinematography acquired through partnership/licensing. Practical effects, real locations, smaller stunt complement.

Distribution: Festival debut → boutique theatrical → streaming acquisition. Sundance, TIFF, Toronto, SXSW, Telluride.

Tier 2 · Premium
$15M to $35M

Streaming-tier flagship. A-list-adjacent lead. Real S-76 sequences. Practical helicopter aerials over ocean and Hispaniola terrain. Cap-Haïtien recreation in DR or Bahamas. Stunt-coordinated tarmac sequence.

Distribution: Major streamer commission or wide-release theatrical with downstream streaming. Targets: Apple, Netflix, Amazon, A24, Searchlight, Focus. Awards-eligible.

Tier 3 · Theatrical Studio
$45M to $80M+

Wide-release thriller. A-list lead. Larger ensemble. Multiple aerial unit days. Practical helicopter destruction or near-destruction beats. Set-piece tarmac confrontation. Studio-grade sound design and score.

Distribution: Theatrical wide release with international territorial sales. Studio packaging. Targets: Skydance, MRC, Universal, Warner, Apple Original Films, Amazon MGM.

Budget Discipline Note: The story does not require Tier 3 economics to work. The operational realism, the geography, the cockpit tension, and the protagonist's voice all play at any tier. The recommendation for the scripted feature is Tier 2 ($15M to $35M): enough budget to do the helicopter and tarmac work practically, with star casting that elevates festival and streaming positioning, while keeping the production lean enough that international territorial sales can underwrite a meaningful percentage of the negative cost.

Recommended Pitch

Lead with Tier 2 ($15M to $35M) as the primary scripted feature posture. Position Tier 1 as a fallback for indie financiers and Tier 3 as the upside case for studio/streamer flagship interest. The documentary, Across the Blue, is already financed through picture lock and is the creative and audience proof point that makes the scripted conversation real.

Financed

The documentary is financed. A single financier and rights holder funds the production all-in through picture lock, released in three tranches tied to the three shoots. The allocation below covers fees, all three trips, insurance and legal, the edit, and contingency inside one number. Finishing beyond picture lock, color, mix, original music, archival licensing, and release costs, is scoped as a second round once the edit is underway.

Tranche 1 · Trip 1
Months 1–2

Released on execution. Covers pre-production, the small-crew foundation shoot, and the anchor interview. The footage that sketches the bigger production.

Tranche 2 · Trip 2
Months 3–4

The largest tranche. Full crew, the principal interview at master production value, and every remaining core interview. The assembly edit begins from this material.

Outcome: The narrative spine of the film is in the can and cutting.

Tranche 3 · Trip 3 + Edit
Months 5–9

Aerials, visuals, atmosphere, and pickup interviews, then the edit carried through picture lock. Cost reporting after each trip; any material overage requires mutual approval.

The Waterfall

The financier recoups first from all revenues. After recoupment, ownership and net proceeds split 50/50 between financier and filmmaker across all media, including any scripted adaptation. Creative control sits with the director; sale and distribution decisions require both signatures. Clean, simple, and aligned: nobody profits until the money that made the film comes home.

The Ask

Investor & Buyer Pitch

In the Bahamas, paradise is also geography, and geography is the enemy. 700 islands. 3 hospitals. A man who spent 28 years flying for the New Jersey State Police buys a small charter company, discovers there is no helicopter air ambulance serving the Caribbean, and builds one. His first call isn't from the Bahamas. It's from a U.S. congressman. Ten Americans are trapped in a war zone in Haiti. Could he get them out? He says yes. The Haitian government calls him an international fugitive. He goes back. He extracts seven hundred more.

This is a real story, with real access, a real protagonist, and a visual world that almost no other project has: turquoise water, Sikorsky S-76 helicopters, storm fronts, open-water flying, cockpit tension, and the constant friction of jurisdiction, bureaucracy, weather, and time. It is one documentary, financed and in motion, with a scripted feature waiting inside it. The protagonist is alive. The aircraft are flying. The next mission is tomorrow morning.

Commercial Hook

Cross-border helicopter rescue thriller. Argo meets The Rescue. Premium streaming-tier flagship with theatrical-eligible spectacle.

Prestige Hook

A premium feature documentary in the lineage of The Rescue, Free Solo, and Touching the Void: real systems, real terrain, real human capability against an indifferent environment.

"Saving a life starts to look a lot like smuggling one."

For Financiers

A financed documentary with built-in distribution optionality: festival, streamer, YouTube, or hybrid paths all remain open until the cut exists. The scripted feature adaptation plays at three viable budget tiers as the upside case. Founder participation, real-mission access, and operational authenticity de-risk production. Title and protagonist have international thriller appeal.

For Buyers / Streamers

A premium operational story in a geography no other project owns. Cinematic aircraft for spectacle. Cross-border geopolitics for prestige. Real protagonist for press. The offer is clean: one finished documentary, plus the option to develop the scripted feature adaptation from the same rights package.

Build Plan

Production Thought Starters

A working set of starting points for the production team, the director(s), and the producing partners. These are not finished plans. They are deliberately compact, each one a hook that the team can pull and develop further as the project moves into commissioning, financing, and pre-production.

01 · Production Approach
Operational Realism Over Spectacle
Build the schedule around live-mission access where ethically possible. Capture cockpit, dispatch, and turnaround footage during real operations under signed protocols. Reserve staged sequences for the high-risk beats (Haiti, tarmac confrontation) that cannot be safely captured live. The aim is documentary-grade authenticity inside a premium-grade frame.
02 · Visual Language
Turquoise / Blackwater / Instrument Glow
Three palettes carry the project: daylight Caribbean (turquoise, white sand, cumulus), blackwater night (cobalt, void, bioluminescence), and cockpit interior (green and amber instrument glow on a charcoal cabin). Cinematic anamorphic for scripted; documentary-grade Sony / ARRI handheld for nonfiction. No drone-only sequences without grounded handheld coverage to anchor them.
03 · Locations & Unit Strategy
Florida / Bahamas / DR Triangle
Primary production base: Fort Lauderdale Executive Airport. Secondary aerial unit: Nassau, Exuma, Eleuthera. Reenactment locations: Dominican Republic stand-ins for Cap-Haïtien arrival; Bahamian airfields for Turks & Caicos middle-of-the-night beats. Avoid live shooting in Haiti unless security and clearance posture changes meaningfully.
04 · Archival & Research
Paper Trail as Spine
Marinello has retained mission logs, flight plans, dispatch records, photographs, and personal correspondence from the Haiti operation. The first research priority: catalog and clear that archive. Secondary: NJSP records (where releasable), Sikorsky press archives, U.S. State Department open-source advisories, FAA registry, congressional liaison correspondence (with consent).
05 · Aircraft & Aviation Realism
FAA-Compliant from Day One
Engage a dedicated aviation coordinator and FAA-certified aerial cinematography unit. All scripted aerial work flown under Motion Picture Manual provisions. No "movie magic" cockpit choreography that conflicts with how an S-76 is actually flown. Sikorsky press relations engaged early for IP, aircraft access, and technical accuracy review.
06 · Safety & Legal Review
Two Lanes: Production Safety, Editorial Liability
Production safety: stunt coordinator, on-set medic, aviation insurance specialist, ocean-water rescue protocol. Editorial liability: defamation review on every named individual; sensitivity review on every Haitian, Bahamian, and Dominican depiction; consent and likeness paperwork on every patient or rescued civilian who appears on screen.
07 · Music & Sound
Engine, Air, Silence, Sub-Bass
Score posture: minimal melodic intervention. The Pratt & Whitney engine note, the rotor wash, the headset chatter, and the silence inside a cockpit at 8,000 feet are the score. Where score enters, lean low and slow: cellos, sub-bass drone, occasional motif tied to the protagonist. Avoid "thrilling" library cues that betray the tone.
08 · Trailer / Sizzle Strategy
Cut for the Buyer Before the Audience
Two sizzles: a 90-second buyer-facing piece built around the Haiti extraction (geography, stakes, founder), and a 30-second public-facing teaser built around the visual world (turquoise, helicopter, cockpit, night crossing). The 90-second cut is the financing weapon; the 30-second cut is the audience opener.
09 · Pitch Materials
Three Decks, One Spine
A 12-page documentary deck (founder-led, mission-driven). A 16-page scripted deck (logline, comps, casting wishlist, budget tiers). Both keep the same visual identity, so a buyer who sees one already knows the universe.
10 · Partnership Targets
Aviation, Maritime, First-Responder
Sikorsky / Lockheed Martin (aircraft IP and access), Pratt & Whitney (engine sequences), Florida Division of Emergency Management (Haiti operational record), select aviation media (AirMed&Rescue, Vertical, Rotor & Wing). NGO-adjacent partnerships only where the partnership advances the story without exploiting the rescued.

Sundance / SXSW / Telluride
Documentary-side festival posture: Sundance (premier), SXSW (premiere alternate), Telluride (prestige), Toronto Documentaries (international). Markets: AFI Docs, Hot Docs (industry), DOC NYC, IDFA. Scripted-side festival path runs through TIFF, Toronto Special Presentations, and Venice / Telluride combos.

Streamer-First, Theatrical-Eligible

TikTok / Reels / Shorts: 30-90 second cockpit, dispatch, and rescue beats. Procedural / Aviation / Premium Doc
Core: viewers of The Rescue, Free Solo, Top Gun: Maverick, Argo, 13 Hours, premium aviation and maritime YouTube channels. Adjacent: subscribers to The Atlantic, Outside, GQ long-form journalism. Underserved: Caribbean-resident audiences, expat/yachting communities, and international viewers in fragmented-archipelago geographies.

Each hook seeds a different press category: trade, aviation, geopolitical, lifestyle, business, entertainment.
16 · Reenactment Strategy
Labeled, Limited, Loyal to Record
Reenactment used only for sequences that cannot be captured live (the original Haiti extraction, the tarmac confrontation, the closed-airport arrival). Always labeled on screen. Pre-delivery: full E&O insurance review, defamation pass, sensitivity pass, and a final rights audit of every named person, branded artifact, archival second, and identifiable face.
